Roofline Case Study: Four Kernels on the B200

Companion to GPU Perf Lesson 1: The Roofline Model, vLLM Study Group. Measured 2026-11-21 on NVIDIA B200 GPUs.

1Question

The roofline model claims that two numbers, a kernel's arithmetic intensity and its achieved performance, suffice to say which resource limits it and how much it could still gain. This study tests the claim on four kernels chosen to occupy the four characteristic positions on a roofline: a memory-bound kernel on its roof, a memory-bound kernel well beneath it, and one matrix multiplication measured at two batch sizes, once on each side of the ridge point.

2Method

The two element-wise kernels are small Triton kernels written for this study after the modulation operations of MiniMax-H3, operating on 32,768 rows of 5,376 BF16 elements. The matrix multiplication is a decode-shaped cuBLAS GEMM: M token vectors of length 8,192 multiplied by a BF16 weight of 8,192 × 28,672 (470 MB, larger than the 126 MB L2, so that every launch reads it from DRAM). Nsight Compute profiled one launch of each case with the full section set on one B200, after one warm-up launch outside the profiled range.

Each point on the roofline is computed from three quantities. The bytes are the DRAM reads and writes measured by Nsight Compute. The time is the kernel duration measured by Nsight Compute. The operations are counted from the algorithm (2MKN for the GEMM, 2 and 6 per element for the gate and the norm), because Nsight Compute's BF16 tensor-operation counters read zero for these Blackwell cuBLAS kernels although their tensor pipes were 73 to 82 percent busy; the profiler's own roofline chart would therefore place the GEMM far below its true position. The memory roof is the peak Nsight Compute reports for the device, 1.92 kB per DRAM cycle at 3.995 GHz, or 7.67 TB/s. The compute roof is NVIDIA's figure for dense BF16, 2.25 PFLOP/s, which places the ridge point at 293 FLOP/byte.

4The roofline

5Reading the roofline

Points 1 and 2: same side, different distance.

Both element-wise kernels lie two to three decades left of the ridge, so both are memory-bound: no change to their arithmetic could make them faster. They differ in their distance from the roof. The column-tiled gate reaches 86% of the roof and has little left to gain. The norm kernel reaches only 42%. Its intensity is slightly higher, so its roof is higher, yet it delivers less: it uses 74 registers per thread, which limits theoretical occupancy to 37.5 percent and leaves too few warps to cover memory latency. The vertical gap between a point and the roof is the opportunity, and here it identifies the norm kernel as the one worth optimizing.

Points 3 and 4: one operation on both sides of the ridge.

The decode GEMM with one token has an intensity of about 1 FLOP/byte, no higher than an element-wise kernel. It is memory-bound and runs at 85% of the memory roof: its time is the time to read the 470 MB weight. With 4,096 tokens the same multiplication reuses each weight tile across many rows, its intensity rises to about 1,050 FLOP/byte, and it moves to the compute side of the ridge at 72% of the compute roof. Nsight Compute shows the hand-over directly: DRAM throughput falls from 85 to 20 percent of peak and SM throughput rises from 25 to 97 percent. The shortfall from the compute roof at M = 4,096 reflects the clock, which ran at 1.39 GHz under sustained tensor-core load.

What the chart does not show.

The roofline locates a kernel; it does not explain it. Why the norm kernel sits low required the occupancy and register data in Nsight Compute's Launch Statistics and Occupancy sections. Why the large GEMM's intensity is 1,050 and not the 2,493 its operand sizes suggest required the measured bytes of Section 6.

Table 2 The minimum DRAM traffic each kernel must generate, against the traffic Nsight Compute measured.

For cases 1 to 3 the measured reads match the minimum to within a megabyte: each input element is read once, and the small gate and norm tables are served from L2. Measured writes fall 30 to 43 MB short for the element-wise kernels because L2 is a write-back cache and the last part of each output reaches DRAM after the kernel ends; the 4 MB written by case 3 are the split-K partial sums. Case 4 reads three times its minimum, because its operands do not fit in L2 together and tiles are fetched more than once. Using the minimum would have placed case 4 at 2,493 FLOP/byte instead of 1,050. The conclusion would not change here, but a gap of this kind is worth checking whenever a kernel's position on the roofline matters.

7Limitations

  • The element-wise kernels are simplified stand-ins written for teaching, not the vLLM-Omni implementations.
  • These are isolated launches. Inside a model, memory-bound kernels run slower than in isolation.
  • Nsight Compute replays each kernel many times and controls clocks between passes, so its durations differ by a few percent from free-running timings.
  • The compute roof is a vendor figure at boost clock. The achieved clock under load was lower, so the effective compute roof for case 4 was lower than drawn.
